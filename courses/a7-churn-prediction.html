Part 1 · covers label/feature windows + the leakage traps

The framing IS the model 10 min live

Before any algorithm, you make one decision that determines whether the model is real: pick a cutoff time t. Features may use behavior up to and including t. The label is whether the customer churned in a window strictly after t - within 30, 60, or 90 days, matching a1's forward-looking law. Any feature that peeks past the cutoff is leakage: the model aces validation because it is reading the answer key, then dies in production where the future genuinely is not available yet.

LiveSpot the leak: 8 features, 3 of them lie6 min▶

A junior analyst proposes this feature list for a 90-day ecommerce churn model with cutoff t. Three features leak. Find them before reading on - in session we vote before the reveal.

  1. tenure_days - days from first purchase to t
  2. orders_last_90d - orders in the 90 days before t
  3. days_since_last_order - computed when the training script runs (today)
  4. support_tickets_180d - tickets opened in the 180 days before t
  5. exit_survey_score - the churn survey answer, NaN for active customers
  6. payment_failures_90d - failed charges in the 90 days before t
  7. sessions_week_after_cutoff - site visits in the 7 days after t
  8. avg_order_value - mean order value over orders before t
The reveal: 3, 5 and 7 #3 is the classic silent leak: days-since-last computed at training time instead of at the cutoff encodes everything that happened AFTER t - churners trivially have huge values. Compute it as (t - last order before t). #5 is a field only populated at churn: exit-survey answers, final-invoice flags, cancellation reasons - the label wearing a costume. If a column is NaN for exactly the customers who did not churn, it is the answer key. #7 is post-cutoff activity, the label window itself. The other five are clean because every one of them can be computed on the day t falls, using only the past.
★ Build the windowed training frame - the cutoff does all the workimport pandas as pd events = pd.read_csv("data/ecommerce_orders.csv", parse_dates=["order_date"]) cutoff = pd.Timestamp("2011-06-01") past = events[events["order_date"] <= cutoff] # feature window future = events[(events["order_date"] > cutoff) & (events["order_date"] <= cutoff + pd.Timedelta(days=90))] feats = past.groupby("customer_id").agg( tenure_days = ("order_date", lambda s: (cutoff - s.min()).days), orders_90d = ("order_date", lambda s: (s > cutoff - pd.Timedelta(days=90)).sum()), days_since_last = ("order_date", lambda s: (cutoff - s.max()).days), avg_order_value = ("amount", "mean"), ) feats["churned_90d"] = (~feats.index.isin(future["customer_id"])).astype(int)

One frame per cutoff. Every aggregate above reads only past; the label reads only future. That physical separation - two dataframes, not one - is the cheapest leakage insurance there is.

Part 2 · covers baseline-then-boost, imbalance, SHAP

Baseline, boost, explain ★ 12 min · everyone builds

Always fit logistic regression first: its coefficients are a sanity check (does more tenure really lower risk? do payment failures raise it?) and a floor the fancy model must beat. Then XGBoost or LightGBM as the workhorse. And because churn is imbalanced, accuracy is a lie: on 5% churn, a model that predicts "nobody churns" is 95% accurate and catches zero churners. Precision-recall is the honest lens - precision is your wasted intervention budget, recall is your missed churners.

Beat THAT, not 0.5.
★ SHAP: global drivers, then one customer's storyimport shap explainer = shap.TreeExplainer(model) shap_values = explainer(X_te) shap.summary_plot(shap_values, X_te) # beeswarm: which features drive risk shap.plots.waterfall(shap_values[42]) # why THIS customer scores 0.83

The beeswarm answers the stakeholder question ("what drives churn here?") once, globally. The waterfall answers the CRM question ("why is this customer flagged?") per row - that per-customer explanation is what makes a score actionable instead of spooky.

Self-studyFeature ideas that survive production4 min read▶

The features that keep working are the boring ones any pipeline can recompute at any historical cutoff:

  • Tenure at the cutoff - months since first purchase or install.
  • Usage trend deltas - this week's activity minus last week's, week-over-week, computed inside the feature window. A falling delta is often the earliest honest signal.
  • Support tickets - count and recency before the cutoff; pair with resolution time if you have it.
  • Payment failures - failed charges before the cutoff; among the strongest involuntary-churn predictors.
  • Days-since-last, computed at the cutoff - the same recency that powers a6's BG/NBD, and this session's #1 leak when computed lazily at training time.

Rule of thumb: for every feature, ask "could the data warehouse have produced this exact value on the morning of the cutoff date?" If the answer needs the word "today", it leaks.

Part 3 · covers the Ascarza caveat + intervention economics

Prediction is not prevention 8 min live

Here is the finding that should be stapled to every churn model, from Eva Ascarza's research: the customers most likely to churn are often the least persuadable. Ranking by risk and mailing the top decile wastes budget twice - on "lost causes" no offer can save, and on "sure things" who would have stayed anyway. What moves money is targeting persuadables: customers whose behavior actually changes because you contacted them. That is uplift modeling - two models (treated vs control) or one model with a treatment flag - and it needs experiment data, which is why this course hands the intervention design to learn-experimentation-with-phoebe.

LiveThe four customer types - and the one worth your budget5 min▶

Cross two counterfactuals - what they do if left alone, what they do if contacted - and every customer lands in one of four boxes:

  • Sure things - stay either way. Every discount sent here is margin set on fire.
  • Persuadables - churn if ignored, stay if contacted. The ONLY box where the offer causes retention. All budget goes here.
  • Lost causes - churn either way. Usually the top of your risk ranking, which is exactly the trap.
  • Sleeping dogs - stay if ignored, churn BECAUSE you contacted them. A renewal reminder that wakes someone who forgot they were subscribed. Do not disturb.

And the threshold question changes shape: not "which cutoff maximizes F1" but which cutoff maximizes (margin saved per rescued customer) minus (cost of offer x customers contacted). A $5 winback email tolerates low precision; a $50 discount does not. Price the threshold from the intervention economics, then read the precision-recall curve at that price.

Real world

Winback economics, priced honestly. Reactivation emails realistically win back 10-30% of lapsed customers - a real but bounded lever. The combination that works in practice: a7's risk score says who is fading, a6's P(alive) says who is still plausibly alive, and a4's value segments say who is worth an offer. Send the winback to high-value, fading-but-alive customers - not to the whole top of the risk ranking, where the lost causes live and the budget goes to die.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · A colleague splits the churn training frame with train_test_split(shuffle=True) and reports a stellar validation score. Why is the split itself a leak?

Churn frames are time-stamped: customers observed under the same market conditions, promotions and seasonality end up in both train and test, so the model is graded on a period it effectively trained on. Split by time - train on older cutoffs, test on a strictly later one - so validation simulates deployment.

2 · Your churn rate is 5% and a model reports 95% accuracy. What have you learned about the model?

On imbalanced classes, accuracy mostly measures the class ratio. Precision-recall is the honest lens: precision prices wasted intervention budget, recall prices missed churners, and PR-AUC should be compared against the churn rate itself (a random ranker's PR-AUC), not against 0.5.

3 · Under uplift thinking, who gets the retention offer?

Ascarza's finding: the riskiest customers are often lost causes no offer can save, and blanket saves also pay sure things who would stay anyway - while sleeping dogs churn BECAUSE you contacted them. Only persuadables convert offer spend into retained revenue, and only experiment data can identify them.
