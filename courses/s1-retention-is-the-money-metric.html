Part 1 · covers the leaky bucket and the compounding base

The leaky bucket vs the compounding base 15 min live

Picture your active customers as water in a bucket. Acquisition pours in at the top; churn leaks out the bottom. The uncomfortable physics: the leak rate, not the pour rate, sets how full the bucket can ever get. A business acquiring furiously with weak retention is not growing - it is running on a treadmill, paying every month to replace the customers it lost last month. A business with strong retention turns the same acquisition spend into a base that stacks, layer on layer.

LiveThe arithmetic of leaks: same spend, opposite trajectories6 min▶

Two companies each acquire 10,000 new users every month, at the same cost. Company L keeps 80% of its base month over month; Company C keeps 95%. That 15-point gap sounds modest. Run it forward and it is the whole story:

  • The ceiling is fixed by the leak. A bucket fills until outflow equals inflow: steady-state MAU = monthly new users divided by monthly churn rate. Company L tops out at 10,000 / 0.20 = 50,000 users, forever, no matter how long it keeps spending. Company C's ceiling is 10,000 / 0.05 = 200,000. Same pour, 4x the pond.
  • After 12 months Company L sits near 47,000 - already 93% of everything it will ever be. Company C is near 92,000 and still climbing steeply, with years of growth left on the same budget.
  • The treadmill test: if next quarter's growth plan is "raise acquisition," ask what the churn rate is first. Pouring faster into a bucket that leaks 20% a month buys a higher plateau, not growth - and the moment spend pauses, the water line falls.

This is why retention is the money metric: it is the multiplier on every acquisition dollar you will ever spend. The numbers above are illustrative arithmetic - in session s2 you will read this exact story off a real cohort chart.

Self-studyRetention precedes monetization: the free-to-play lesson4 min read▶

Mobile games make the sequencing brutally clear, because their revenue is voluntary. In free-to-play, only 2-5% of players ever pay anything at all. And the money is astonishingly concentrated: the top 10% of paying players generate roughly 64% of revenue, and the top 1% about 29% (Swrve's payer analysis).

  • Whales are not found, they are grown. A big spender is almost never big in week one. High-value players emerge over weeks and months of play - which means they can only emerge from the pool of players who stayed. Kill retention and you have mathematically capped monetization before the first offer is shown.
  • The ecommerce echo: the same concentration shows up in shops. Returning customers are typically around 21% of a store's customers but around 44% of its revenue. The repeat buyer is the business.
  • The sequencing rule for your roadmap: monetization work multiplies the retained base; it cannot substitute for one. When a team proposes squeezing more revenue from a shrinking base, the arithmetic of Part 1 already told you how that ends.
Part 2 · covers Reichheld 1990 + the honest 5-25x

The real numbers behind the famous claims 12 min live

Two lines appear in nearly every retention deck ever written: "improving retention 5% raises profits 25-95%" and "keeping a customer is 5-25x cheaper than acquiring one." One of these has a landmark study behind it. The other has a blog post. Knowing which is which - and quoting each accordingly - is the cheapest credibility upgrade available to a leader.

LiveOne claim is documented, the other is directional - treat them differently6 min▶

The documented one. Reichheld & Sasser, Harvard Business Review, 1990 ("Zero Defections"). They measured what happened to per-customer profit as tenure grew, across service industries, and found that cutting customer defections by 5 percentage points raised profits 25-85% depending on the industry (later Bain & Company work stretched the range to 25-95%). The mechanism is boring and durable: long-tenured customers cost less to serve, spend more, and refer. This claim you can quote with a straight face - with its year attached.

The soft one. "It is 5-25x cheaper to retain a customer than to acquire one" traces to a 2014 HBR piece that cites no underlying study. A range that wide is a shrug in numeric clothing. The direction is almost certainly right - acquisition has a real, rising price tag and retention work is often cheaper - but it is folklore wearing a lab coat. Teach your teams to use it as a directional prior, never as a measured constant in a business case.

The one-sentence versions for your next deck Documented: "Reichheld and Sasser, 1990: a 5-point cut in defections raised profits 25-85% across the industries studied." Directional: "Retaining is usually far cheaper than acquiring - the popular 5-25x figure is a directional range, not a measured constant." Saying the second sentence honestly makes people trust your first one more. That is the whole trick of evidence hygiene.
Part 3 · covers LTV:CAC, quick ratio, NRR + the envelope math

The three money translations 12 min live

Retention percentages do not move budgets; dollar figures do. Three ratios translate retention into money, and each has a specific moment in a meeting when it is the right thing to ask about. You do not need to compute any of them - you need to know what they mean and when to summon them.

LiveLTV:CAC, quick ratio, NRR - one sentence each6 min▶
  • LTV:CAC - for every dollar spent acquiring a customer, how many dollars of lifetime value come back; the classic healthy benchmark is 3:1 (David Skok). Retention is the engine of the LTV half: customers who stay longer are worth more, so every retention point raises the ratio without touching ad spend. When to ask: the moment anyone proposes scaling acquisition - "what is LTV:CAC on that channel, and what retention assumption is inside the LTV?"
  • Quick ratio - users gained for every user lost: (new + resurrected) / churned. For consumer products, 1.5-2.0 is very good. It is the leaky bucket as a single number: below 1 the bucket is draining even if the top line looks busy. When to ask: whenever MAU is flat and nobody can say why - the quick ratio splits "not filling" from "leaking".
  • NRR (net revenue retention) - revenue this period from last period's customers, as a percent of what they paid before. Above 100% means the existing base grows by itself: the business would grow with zero new sales. When to ask: any subscription or B2B review - "if we hired no salespeople next year, does revenue rise or fall?"

Notice what all three have in common: acquisition can flatter each of them for a quarter, but only retention can hold them up for years.

Find the retention number your company already reports. Ask which definition it uses and whether historical values ever restate (session s2 explains why that question matters so much).

Get the size of one recent monthly cohort - new customers or new players in a single month.

Multiply: 5 points × cohort size × ARPU. Write the monthly dollar figure down before the meeting ends.

Open your next planning conversation with it: "a retention point is worth roughly $X a month per cohort - what would it cost to buy one?"

Real world

Amazon Prime: the retention machine, quoted honestly. CIRP estimates that roughly 93% of Prime members renew after their first year, and about 98% renew after two - and that members spend around twice what non-members do. Two caveats, and the caveats are the teaching: these are third-party survey estimates (Amazon does not publish them), and the 2x spend is partly a selection effect - heavy shoppers are the ones who join Prime in the first place. Retention numbers that good always deserve the question "who selected in?" Ask it even when - especially when - the number supports your case.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · What did the Reichheld & Sasser 1990 study actually find?

The 1990 HBR study measured profit against customer tenure across service industries: a 5-point cut in defections lifted profits 25-85% (bank deposits at the top, auto service near the bottom; later Bain work stretched to 25-95%). The "5x cheaper" line is a different, unsourced claim - and "always 2x spend" is the Prime estimate, which is partly selection effect.

2 · Why should the famous "5-25x cheaper to retain than acquire" be quoted carefully?

The direction (retention is usually cheaper) is almost certainly right, but the number has no study behind it and a range that wide is a shrug. Quote it as a directional prior; build business cases on your own cohort math instead - delta retention × cohort size × ARPU.

3 · Why does retention precede monetization in free-to-play games?

F2P revenue is voluntary and concentrated: 2-5% pay at all, and the top 10% of payers drive ~64% of revenue (Swrve). High-value players are grown over weeks of play, not found on day one - so a leaky base mathematically caps monetization before any offer is shown.
