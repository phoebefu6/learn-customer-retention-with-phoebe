Part 1 · covers the cohort triangle + the frozen-history law

The cohort triangle in thirty seconds 15 min live

The triangle looks intimidating and is actually three rules. Rows are join months - everyone who became a customer in January shares the January row forever. Columns are months since joining - month 0 is the join month itself, so it is 100% by construction. And the empty corner is the future, not failure - June's month-5 simply has not happened yet. Anyone painting those empty cells as zeros is reporting on events that have not occurred.

LiveThe three scan directions: a thirty-second reading, every time6 min▶

You never need to read every cell. Three sweeps of the eyes extract everything the triangle knows:

  • Across a row - one class of customers aging. The January row is the January joiners' whole life story: 100% at month 0, then the fade. Where the fade stops mattering more than where it starts - that is the curve shape, and Part 2 is entirely about it.
  • Down a column - are newer classes better? Down the month-1 column, every cohort is compared at the exact same age, so this is the cleanest "is the product improving" read that exists. 45, 47, 50, 54 going down means every new class of customers sticks better than the last - whatever changed since January is working.
  • Along a diagonal - calendar shocks. Cells on one diagonal share a calendar month. A dark or light stripe along a diagonal means something hit everyone at once regardless of age: an outage, a price change, a holiday season. (Its cousin: a vertical stripe means something hits every cohort at the same age - like a paywall that appears in month 2.)

The order matters. Across tells you what kind of business you have, down tells you whether it is getting better, diagonal tells you what the calendar did to it. Run all three before anyone starts explaining the chart to you, and you will usually be done before they are.

Frozen history, in executive terms Every number you should accept in a readout is computed by fixing a group at its join date and looking forward. The payoff for you: a completed cell never changes. January's month-2 number is the same today, next quarter, and in the board pack two years from now, which is what makes targets and comparisons possible at all. The only legitimately moving cells are the current month's (outlined in the figure, always visibly marked as partial). So if last quarter's number is different in this quarter's deck, do not let it slide: either the metric secretly re-anchors to today, or a returning user is rewriting history retroactively - neither belongs in any readout, and neither should survive the meeting unquestioned.
Part 2 · covers curve shapes as verdicts + the PMF read

The curve is the verdict 12 min live

Take any row of the triangle and lay it flat and you get the retention curve: percent still active, month by month. Only three shapes exist, and each one is a verdict on the business - not a nuance, a verdict. Learning to name the shape out loud is the single highest-leverage chart skill on this track.

LiveWhat to say when you see each shape6 min▶

The shapes come with decision language. Practice these sentences; they are the difference between reading a chart and using one.

  • Slide to zero: "Pause the acquisition scale-up - every cohort we buy is fully evaporating. The money belongs in onboarding and core product until this curve grows a floor." A sliding curve means no product-market fit for that audience yet; traffic cannot fix it, only feed it.
  • Flatten above zero: "We have a durable base - each cohort leaves permanent residents. Now acquisition spend buys compounding assets, so this is the moment to scale it. And the floor height is the KPI: what moves it?" This is the classic PMF read (Sequoia's retention essays): a flattened curve of the key action at its natural frequency, plus month-over-month new-customer growth, is what product-market fit actually looks like in data (Casey Winters' formulation).
  • The smile: "Something is resurrecting churned customers - find out what, and bottle it." Smiles are rare and precious: seasonal products, winback campaigns that land, or a feature that pulls dormant users back. Verify it is real resurrection and not a definition quirk, then fund whatever causes it.

One discipline ties all three together: acquisition spend is gated on the flatten. It is the boardroom version of "retention precedes monetization" from s1 - retention also precedes scale.

Self-studyThe flattening rows behind a $1B story: Dollar Shave Club3 min read▶

When Unilever paid $1B for Dollar Shave Club in 2016, the headline was the viral video. The diligence story was shape 2. Second Measure's transaction data showed DSC's cohort curves flattening at roughly 50% retention at six months - subscriber classes that fell and then stayed, cohort after cohort, leading its whole category.

  • Why that shape is worth a billion dollars: a flattening curve means each acquired cohort leaves a permanent layer of recurring revenue. Stack the layers and revenue compounds even if marketing never improves - the "durable base" verdict, priced.
  • Why the triangle beat the topline: topline revenue growth can be bought with spend (s1's treadmill). Flattening cohort rows cannot be faked that way - they show what customers do after the coupon is spent. This is why sophisticated acquirers and investors read cohort charts, not just revenue charts.
  • Your takeaway as a leader: when you want to know if a business - yours or one you are buying - has durable revenue, ask for the cohort curves and look for the flatten. It is the same read at every scale.

One more way to make the shapes land in a boardroom: forget percentages for a moment and walk one hundred actual customers through their first two months.

Part 3 · covers 40/20/10 folklore vs GameAnalytics 2025 + ecommerce yardsticks

Benchmarks with their vintage 10 min live

Someone will eventually tell you "good games do 40/20/10" - 40% day-1, 20% day-7, 10% day-30 retention. That rule is real folklore with a real past, but today it marks top-quartile-or-better territory, not average. The GameAnalytics 2025 study across 11,600 games (1.48B monthly players) puts the medians far lower: day-1 around 22-27%, day-7 around 3.4-3.9%, and 75% of games under 3% by day 28. A realistic "good" profile in 2026 looks more like 35/15/5. Ecommerce has the same trap in different clothes: the cross-industry average repeat purchase rate is about 28%, and roughly 76% of second orders arrive within 90 days - so a shop comparing itself to a grocery-style repeat rate is benchmarking against the wrong species.

LiveNever accept a naked benchmark4 min▶

A benchmark without a year and a source population is a rumor with an axis. Before any number becomes a target, make it answer three questions:

  • What year is it from? 40/20/10 dates to the early free-to-play era (its exact attribution is murky even in the industry). Markets saturate, install quality changes, and a 2013 top-quartile bar quoted as a 2026 average will set your team an impossible target - then teach them to hit it by redefining the metric.
  • Who is in the population? Puzzle games and hyper-casual games have wildly different profiles even within one 2025 study - and a games benchmark means nothing for a shop. For ecommerce, use ecommerce yardsticks: repeat purchase rate near the ~28% cross-industry average, and second orders concentrated inside 90 days.
  • Which definition of retention? Session s1's homework question returns: a "day-7" computed on all installs vs activated users, or on a rolling instead of fixed-window basis, moves the number enormously with zero change in the business.

The sentence to keep: "What is the vintage and the population on that benchmark?" Asking it once, publicly, permanently upgrades the quality of numbers people bring you.

Real world

Supercell: retention as a kill gate. The studio behind Clash of Clans soft-launches every game in small markets and judges it against the retention bars of its live hits - and kills it if the cohorts fall short. In 2015 alone it killed Spooky Pop and Smash Land in soft launch (Smash Land despite positive player feedback), and it has killed 30+ titles over the years. Notice what is gating what: the marketing budget does not rescue weak retention - weak retention cancels the marketing budget. That governance pattern travels far beyond games: no scale-up spend until the cohort chart clears the bar.

Open your product's cohort chart and run the three scans in order: across the newest complete row, down the month-1 column, then along the diagonals.

Name the curve shape out loud - slide, flatten, or smile - and say the decision sentence that goes with it.

Find the benchmark your team currently uses and ask for its year and its source population. If nobody knows, you have found this week's most useful question.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 20 min total

  • Check whether any empty future cells are being displayed as zeros or dark cells anywhere in your reporting. If yes, that is a bug worth an email today.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Reading DOWN a column of the cohort triangle, the numbers rise: 45, 47, 50, 54. What does that mean?

Down a column, every cohort is compared at the SAME age, so cohort-over-cohort improvement is the cleanest read of whether changes are working. One cohort recovering would show across its own row; a calendar event would show as a diagonal stripe.

2 · Which curve shape is the data signature of product-market fit?

The flatten is the verdict: a retention curve of the key action that levels off above zero, combined with growing new-customer counts, is the PMF read (Casey Winters / Sequoia). A steep start can coexist with a slide to zero, and signup growth alone is the s1 treadmill.

3 · A partner confidently quotes "good games do 40/20/10." What is the right response?

40/20/10 is real folklore with real history, but today it marks top-quartile-or-better, not average: GameAnalytics 2025 (11,600 games) puts medians far lower, with a realistic good profile near 35/15/5. Benchmarks are useful - naked benchmarks are not. Year and population, always.
