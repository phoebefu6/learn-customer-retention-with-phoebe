Survival analysis and the censoring problem

Your best customers share an annoying statistical property: they have not churned yet. Average the lifetimes of only the customers who quit and you get a number biased low - you measured the quitters and ignored the loyal. Today you meet censoring, build Kaplan-Meier curves that count everyone honestly, test segment gaps with log-rank, and read Cox hazard ratios - all in lifelines, all feeding the retention report you ship in a8.

Part 1 · covers right-censoring + the Kaplan-Meier estimator

The censoring problem 15 min live

A customer who is still active when your data ends has not churned - their lifetime is at least what you observed, and that is all you know. They are right-censored. The naive "average customer lifetime" - mean tenure of the customers who churned - silently throws these people away, so it only counts the quitters and comes out biased low. Kaplan-Meier counts everyone: S(t) is the probability of surviving past t, drawn as a step curve that steps down only at observed churn events. Censored customers simply exit the risk set when their data runs out - they thin the denominator of later steps without stepping the curve themselves.

Live★ Build-along: the six-customer worked example8 min▶

Six customers, observed for up to ten months. A churned at month 2, C at month 4, E at month 7. B, D and F were still active when the data ended, at months 3, 5 and 9 - censored. Walk the estimator by hand once, then let lifelines do it forever:

  • Month 2: 6 customers at risk, 1 churns. S(2) = 5/6 = 0.83. B is censored at month 3 - the curve does not move, but B leaves the risk set.
  • Month 4: 4 at risk (C, D, E, F), 1 churns. S(4) = 0.83 x 3/4 = 0.63. D exits censored at month 5.
  • Month 7: 2 at risk (E, F), 1 churns. S(7) = 0.63 x 1/2 = 0.31. F exits censored at month 9. Median survival - where S(t) crosses 0.5 - is 7 months.
  • The naive number: mean tenure of the churned three is (2 + 4 + 7) / 3 = 4.3 months. The honest median is 7. Same data, and the naive figure understated lifetime by counting only quitters.
★ The same example in lifelines - fit, plot, medianfrom lifelines import KaplanMeierFitter durations = [2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 9] # months each customer was observed event_observed = [1, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0] # 1 = churn seen, 0 = censored (still active) kmf = KaplanMeierFitter() kmf.fit(durations, event_observed=event_observed, label="All customers") kmf.plot_survival_function() # the step curve, with censor ticks print(kmf.median_survival_time_) # 7.0 - not the naive 4.3
But what is a "churn event" for a shop? Contract businesses observe churn: the cancellation lands in a table with a timestamp. Ecommerce and games do not - a quiet customer might be gone or just slow. To run survival on non-subscription data you must define churn as an inactivity window: no purchase for 60 / 90 / 120 days, or a multiple of that customer's own inter-purchase gap. Write the definition down; the homework makes you feel how much the answer moves when it changes.
Part 2 · covers per-segment KM + the log-rank test

Comparing segments honestly 8 min live

One KM curve per segment - contract type, acquisition channel, or the RFM personas from a4 - and the gaps become visible. But visible is not the same as real: two curves from the same population will drift apart by luck. The log-rank test compares whole curves, not one cherry-picked time point, and answers "how surprising is a gap this size if the segments actually churn identically?" This is the censoring-correct cousin of reading a2's triangle rows: same question, but every still-active customer is counted instead of dropped.

Live★ Build-along: two KM fits overlaid + log-rank6 min▶

Split the frame by segment, fit one KaplanMeierFitter per side, plot on a shared axis, then let the test speak. Swap in any a4 persona pair - Champions vs Hibernating is a favorite.

★ Overlay two segments, then test the gapfrom lifelines import KaplanMeierFitter from lifelines.statistics import logrank_test m2m = df[df["contract"] == "month-to-month"] two = df[df["contract"] == "two-year"] ax = KaplanMeierFitter().fit(m2m["tenure_months"], m2m["churned"], label="Month-to-month").plot_survival_function() KaplanMeierFitter().fit(two["tenure_months"], two["churned"], label="Two-year").plot_survival_function(ax=ax) res = logrank_test(m2m["tenure_months"], two["tenure_months"], event_observed_A=m2m["churned"], event_observed_B=two["churned"]) print(res.p_value) # small = the gap is unlikely to be luck

Two reading rules. First, a tiny p-value on a tiny gap can still be commercially boring - report the gap size and the p-value together. Second, if the curves cross, the log-rank test loses power and "which segment is better" depends on the horizon; say so instead of averaging it away.

Part 3 · covers Cox proportional hazards + when not to bother

Cox proportional hazards 14 min live

KM compares one segmentation at a time. Cox proportional hazards handles many covariates at once, and it is semi-parametric: it estimates each covariate's effect as a hazard ratio without ever specifying the baseline churn curve. The canonical Telco read: a month-to-month contract carries roughly 3x the churn hazard of a two-year contract, other covariates held fixed. Read exp(coef) as multiplicative risk, verify the proportional-hazards assumption with check_assumptions(), and judge ranking quality with the C-index.

Live★ Build-along: CoxPHFitter on a Telco-shaped frame8 min▶

One row per customer: tenure in months, a churn flag, and covariates. Fit, print the summary, and read the exp(coef) column out loud before anything else.

★ Fit, read hazard ratios, check the assumption, judge rankingfrom lifelines import CoxPHFitter model_df = telco[["tenure_months", "churned", "contract_m2m", "monthly_charges", "tech_support"]] cph = CoxPHFitter() cph.fit(model_df, duration_col="tenure_months", event_col="churned") cph.print_summary() # exp(coef) column = hazard ratios cph.plot_partial_effects_on_outcome("contract_m2m", values=[0, 1]) cph.check_assumptions(model_df) # proportional-hazards diagnostics print(cph.concordance_index_) # C-index: 0.5 = coin flip, ~0.7+ useful
  • exp(coef) = 3.0 reads: "at any moment, a month-to-month customer who is still with us has three times the instantaneous churn risk of an otherwise-identical two-year customer."
  • Proportional hazards means that ratio is assumed constant over time. check_assumptions() flags covariates that violate it - stratify or add time-varying terms rather than shipping a broken ratio.
  • C-index is the probability the model correctly orders a random pair by churn time - a ranking score, not a calibration score.
Self-studyHazard vs probability, and why models get versions4 min read▶

Hazard is not probability. The hazard at t is the instantaneous churn risk among customers who survived to t - risk per unit time, conditional on still being here. S(t) is the cumulative result of running that gauntlet. A segment can have high early hazard and still contain the most durable survivors, which is exactly the a5 lesson a6 leans on: churn falls with tenure because the fragile leave first.

The frozen-history tie-in. a2's completed triangle cells are facts: frozen, immutable, never restated. Survival estimates are not facts - they are models, and they legitimately update as more customers age and censored lifetimes resolve. So version them: stamp every KM median and hazard ratio with a fit date and a data cutoff, and never splice a re-fit estimate into a frozen reported table as if it were the same kind of number.

When NOT to reach for survival: descriptive reporting where the cohort table already answers the question. Reach for it when censoring is material (many still-active customers), when you need covariate effects, or when you want individual lifetime estimates. A tool upgrade you do not need is just a slower way to say the same thing.

Real world

Subscription and non-subscription businesses are playing different games. A contract business observes churn: the cancellation is an event with a timestamp, so KM and Cox apply directly - which is why Telco churn is the canonical teaching dataset. A shop or a game only ever infers churn from silence, through the inactivity-window definitions from Part 1. That inference gap is precisely why the buy-till-you-die models in a6 exist: they replace "define a window and hope" with a per-customer P(alive).

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Mean tenure of your churned customers is 4.3 months. Why is "average customer lifetime is 4.3 months" wrong?

Still-active customers are right-censored: their lifetimes are at least what you observed, and they are disproportionately your durable customers. Averaging only observed churns systematically understates lifetime. KM keeps them in the risk set and gets the honest answer.

2 · Your Cox summary shows exp(coef) = 3.0 for month-to-month contracts. What does that mean?

A hazard ratio is multiplicative instantaneous risk among survivors, assumed constant over time (that is the proportional-hazards assumption - check it). It is not a difference in months and not a percentage-point gap.

3 · When is the plain a2 cohort table enough, with no survival model at all?

The cohort table is the right tool for describing what happened. Survival earns its complexity when censoring is material, when you need covariate effects (Cox), or when you want per-customer lifetime estimates. Matching tool to question is the skill.
