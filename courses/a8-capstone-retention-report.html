Part 1 · covers the three questions + the one-chart rule

The spec: what a retention report must answer 10 min live

A retention report is not a chart dump. It is a one-page answer to three questions, backed by an appendix, bound by one contract. Write the spec before touching pandas - the spec is what stops the notebook from becoming forty cells of exploration with no verdict.

LiveThe three questions, the frozen contract, the one-chart rule8 min▶

Every retention readout in this course answers exactly three questions, in this order:

  • WHICH cohort? Named precisely: "customers whose first purchase was Feb-May 2011", not "recent customers". The denominator definition (all signups vs activated; purchase-based for ecommerce) is written on the page.
  • WHAT changed, vs what? A number is meaningless without its comparison cohort: "month-3 retention 31% vs 27% for the Dec-Jan cohorts". Cohort ages compared, never calendar periods.
  • HOW MUCH money? Delta retention x cohort size x ARPU. Four points of month-3 retention on 4,000 customers at $30 monthly gross profit is a real number executives can rank against other projects.

And two standing rules bind the whole document:

  • The frozen-history contract: completed months are immutable - this quarter's report must show the SAME numbers for them as last quarter's report, provably. Only the current partial month updates, and it is visibly marked partial. A report whose history restates cannot be trusted, compared, or targeted against. In Part 2 this contract becomes an assert.
  • The one-chart rule: executives get ONE chart - the D30-by-cohort trend line ("are we improving?") or the layer-cake ("is revenue durable?"). The raw triangle, the KM curves, the SHAP beeswarm: appendix. Upstairs answers a question; the appendix proves you did the work.
Part 2 · covers the full pipeline on Online Retail II

Build it end to end ★ 25 min · everyone builds

Six steps from raw invoices to a shippable report. Steps 1-2 are a2's recipe verbatim - if any step feels new, that session is the reference. The new muscle today is steps 5 and 6: translating a finding into money, and writing the frozen-history contract as code that fails loudly.

Clean-up gate. Drop C-prefix invoices (cancellations with negative Quantity), rows missing CustomerID (~135k guest checkouts - despite the metadata claiming none missing), and any remaining negative quantities. Annotate the left-censored first cohort: December 2009 sweeps in pre-existing customers as if new.

Cohort table + masked heatmap. a2's recipe verbatim: stamp, age, count, divide - then the four heatmap choices: annot, mask the future, anchor vmin/vmax, single-hue safe cmap.

Pick the finding. Read down the columns for cohort-over-cohort improvement or deterioration; check the diagonals for calendar shocks (a December spike is seasonality, not genius). One finding. The report is a verdict, not a tour.

Segment the finding. a4's split: is the improvement everyone, or one RFM tier, one channel, one behavior? Wholesalers out first - a handful of weekly trade buyers will dominate any average.

Translate to money. Delta retention x cohort size x ARPU, with ARPU from a6's cumulative cohort gross profit, not a hopeful LTV formula. This line is why the meeting happens.

Snapshot. Write the frozen numbers to a dated CSV and assert that every completed cell matches the prior quarter's snapshot. History restating is now a crash, not a surprise.

Live★ Build-along: the three code moments (steps 2, 5, 6)12 min▶
★ Step 2 · a2's recipe verbatim, on the cleaned Online Retail II frameretail["order_month"] = retail["InvoiceDate"].dt.to_period("M") retail["cohort"] = (retail.groupby("Customer ID")["InvoiceDate"] .transform("min").dt.to_period("M")) retail["period"] = (retail["order_month"] - retail["cohort"]).apply(lambda d: d.n) counts = retail.pivot_table(index="cohort", columns="period", values="Customer ID", aggfunc="nunique") retention = counts.divide(counts.iloc[:, 0], axis=0) sns.heatmap(retention, annot=True, fmt=".0%", mask=retention.isnull(), vmin=0, vmax=0.5, cmap="Oranges") # the four choices, always
★ Step 5 · the money line: delta x size x ARPUimproved = retention.loc["2011-02":"2011-05", 3].mean() # month-3, new cohorts comparison = retention.loc["2010-12":"2011-01", 3].mean() # month-3, old cohorts delta = improved - comparison size = counts.loc["2011-02":"2011-05", 0].sum() # customers in improved cohorts arpu = 30.0 # monthly gross profit per retained customer, from a6's frame impact = delta * size * arpu * 6 # months left in the horizon print(f"{delta:+.1%} at month 3 x {size:,} customers -> ~${impact:,.0f}")
★ Step 6 · snapshot the frozen numbers for next quarter's proofretention.round(4).to_csv("reports/retention_snapshot_2011Q4.csv")

The snapshot is half the contract. The other half - the assert that reads it back next quarter - gets its own card below, because it is the single most transferable pattern on this page.

LiveThe snapshot-assert: the frozen-history contract as code6 min▶

a2 taught frozen history as a law; today it becomes machinery. Before this quarter's report renders, re-read the PRIOR quarter's snapshot and assert that every cell whose window had already completed then is bit-identical now. If the assert fires, something upstream changed - a silent filter edit, a timezone shift, a redefined denominator - and the report must not ship until you know why.

★ The re-read assert - history restating becomes a build failureprior = pd.read_csv("reports/retention_snapshot_2011Q3.csv", index_col=0) prior.columns = prior.columns.astype(int) completed = [c for c in prior.columns if c < prior.shape[1] - 1] # elapsed windows rows = prior.index.intersection(retention.index.astype(str)) new = retention.copy(); new.index = new.index.astype(str) assert new.loc[rows, completed].round(4).equals( prior.loc[rows, completed].round(4)), \ "HISTORY RESTATED - completed cells changed since Q3. Do not ship."

Notice what this buys you politically, not just technically: when a stakeholder asks "why is January's number different from last quarter's deck?", your answer is "it cannot be - the build fails if it is". That sentence is worth more than any model in this course.

Part 3 · covers the ship gate + presenting the verdict

The ship-or-not scorecard 10 min live

A report that fails one row fails. There is no seven-out-of-eight: a missing denominator definition or a restated January quietly poisons every other number on the page. Run the scorecard before every send - on your own report today, and on every retention readout anyone hands you from now on.

LiveEight rows, pass or do-not-ship6 min▶
  1. Which-cohort named - the cohort under discussion is identified by anchor date and definition, not "recent users".
  2. Comparison cohort named - the delta has an explicit baseline cohort, compared at the same age.
  3. Money line present - delta x size x ARPU appears on the page, in currency.
  4. Denominator definition written - all signups vs activated; purchase-based for ecommerce; stated, not assumed.
  5. History matches prior snapshot - the assert ran and passed; completed cells identical to last quarter's report.
  6. Immature cells masked and marked - the current partial month is visibly partial; NaN never rendered as low retention.
  7. Benchmark carries year + source - "GameAnalytics 2025 median D7 ~3.4-3.9%", never a naked "industry says".
  8. One chart upstairs, triangle in appendix - the exec page has exactly one chart; the triangle and diagnostics live behind it.

Frame it exactly this way with your team: ship or do-not-ship. Partial credit is how restated dashboards and unmarked partial months get normalized.

Self-studyPresenting it: the verdict sentence and the two hard questions5 min read▶

Lead with the verdict sentence. Not the methodology, not the caveats: "February-May cohorts retain 4 points better at month 3; worth roughly $X by year end if it holds." Everything else on the page exists to survive the questions that sentence provokes.

  • Anticipate the mix-shift question (s4's trap). "Did retention improve, or did we just acquire stickier customers?" Blended retention can rise while every segment falls if acquisition shifts toward a stickier channel. Your step-4 segment split is the answer - have the by-segment view in the appendix before anyone asks.
  • Keep the answer to WHY honest. Cohort data shows WHEN and WHERE retention changed - it cannot prove why. The behavioral splits and a7's SHAP drivers are hypotheses, and hypotheses get tested with experiments: that handoff belongs to learn-experimentation-with-phoebe, and saying so out loud is what separates a report from a story.
Real world

This deliverable is the Duolingo discipline in miniature. Jorge Mazal's team (in Lenny's Newsletter) ranked candidate metrics by sensitivity analysis, found CURR - current-user retention rate - had roughly 5x the DAU impact of the next metric, made it the north star, and drove decisions from cohort movements against it: CURR +21% and ~4.5x DAU over about four years. One metric chosen deliberately, one chart watched, decisions traced to cohort movements - exactly the shape of the one-pager you just built, at a few thousand times the headcount.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Which chart goes on the executive page, and where does the cohort triangle live?

The triangle is analyst food: it answers twenty questions at once, which is exactly why it answers none for an executive. Upstairs gets the single chart matched to the question at hand ("are we improving?" = trend line; "is revenue durable?" = layer-cake), with the so-what annotated on the chart itself. The triangle proves the work - in the appendix.

2 · What does the snapshot-assert in step 6 actually protect against?

Completed windows are immutable by the course law, so if a completed cell differs from the prior snapshot, something upstream changed - a filter, a timezone, a denominator - and every trend in the report is suspect. The assert turns that silent poison into a loud build failure, and turns "why did January change?" into "it cannot - the build fails if it does".

3 · Your report shows Feb-May cohorts retaining 4 points better after the new onboarding launched. What can the cohort data claim?

Cohort tables are observational: they localize the change precisely in time and segment, which is enormously useful - but the acquisition mix, seasonality, or pricing may have moved too. Behavioral splits and a7's SHAP drivers generate the WHY hypotheses; testing them is experiment design, handed off to learn-experimentation-with-phoebe.
